# Fitness Intelligence System Standards: Sleep, Nutrition, Activity, and Overall Fitness

**Executive Summary:**  Current global health authorities (WHO, CDC, AHA, NHS, AASM, etc.) concur that optimal sleep, nutrition, and physical activity are age- and sex-dependent and critically shape fitness.  For **sleep**, adults generally need ≥7 hours/night (teens 8–10 h, children more), with quality (sleep efficiency, architecture) influenced by sex and hormones (women have more deep sleep but more disturbances).  **Nutrition** guidelines universally emphasize abundant vegetables, fruits, whole grains, lean protein, and limits on sugars, salt, saturated and trans fats.  For example, WHO advises ≥400 g/day fruits/vegetables; free sugars <10% of energy (5% ideal); saturated fat <10% of calories; and an overall fat intake ~15–30% of energy.  Sex and age influence needs: e.g. women need fewer calories (≈2 000 vs 2 500 kcal/day) and more iron (≈18 vs 8 mg/day) than men.  **Physical activity** recommendations are likewise stratified: children ≥60 min moderate/vigorous daily, adults ≥150 min/week moderate (or 75 min vigorous) plus 2+ days muscle-strengthening.  AHA/ACSM echo similar goals.  Men and women have similar activity targets, though actual compliance differs (global inactivity ~31% of adults, higher in women by ~5%; 85% of adolescent girls fail to meet guidelines vs 78% of boys, see chart below).  

**Key domains:**  We compare **sleep, nutrition, activity,** and **general fitness** standards:

- **Sleep:**   Guideline ranges are age-based (e.g. adults ~7–9 h).  No formal sex-specific hours exist, but women often sleep 10–20 min more and have different sleep architecture.  *Metrics:* nightly duration, sleep efficiency (time asleep/time in bed), latency, wake after sleep onset (WASO), and staging (REM/deep%).  *Measurement:* sleep diaries, wrist actigraphy, polysomnography (for detailed architecture).  *Standards:* AASM/CDC recommend ≥7 h for adults; teens 8–10 h.  *Limitations:* Self-reported sleep is imprecise; cultural norms (siestas, shift work) vary.  

- **Nutrition:**  Guidelines focus on **dietary patterns**.  WHO, EAT-Lancet, USDA and others converge on high plant intake, whole grains, and limited added sugars/sodium/saturated fat.  *Metrics:* nutrient intake (g/day of protein, fiber), dietary diversity scores, and index measures (e.g. Healthy Eating Index).  *Standards:* WHO: ≥5 servings fruits/veg (≥400 g) daily; ≤5 g salt/day; sugar <10% energy.  EAT-Lancet’s **Planetary Health Diet** specifies ~210 g whole grains, 300 g vegetables, 200 g fruit, 50 g nuts, 75 g legumes, ≤30 g added sugars daily (per 2400 kcal).  *Sex/Age:* Calorie needs ~25% higher in men; women of childbearing age need extra iron/folate, especially during pregnancy (WHO recommends iron supplementation for all women of reproductive age).  *Measurement:* 24-h dietary recalls, food frequency questionnaires, biomarkers (e.g. serum lipids, vitamin levels).  *Limitations:*  Guideline variation between countries; dietary surveys are laborious and prone to underreporting.  *Sociological:* Healthy eating is constrained by socioeconomic status and culture; many populations exceed sugar/salt limits.  

- **Physical Activity (PA):**  All major bodies (WHO, CDC, AHA) prescribe ≥150–300 min/week moderate exercise for adults, plus muscle-strengthening ≥2 days/week; children need ≥60 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous PA.  *Metrics:* minutes of MVPA per week, steps/day, or MET-minutes, and sedentary time.  Functional tests (e.g. VO₂max or 6-min walk) gauge cardio fitness.  *Sex/Age:* Raw targets are same, but physiology differs: men have ~20–30% higher VO₂max (ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) than women due to larger hearts and hemoglobin; hormonal differences affect muscle adaptation (testosterone in men, estrogen in women).  *Measurement:* accelerometry, pedometers, self-report (IPAQ), fitness tests.  *Standards:* WHO: “any PA is better than none; children 60 min daily; adults 150 min/week”.  *Limitations:* PA recall bias; variations in occupational and transport activities across cultures.  *Sociological:* Globally ~31% of adults inactive (higher in women), reflecting gender roles and access issues (see chart below).  

- **General Fitness & Clinical Metrics:**  Includes body composition, strength, flexibility, balance, and clinical risk factors.  *Metrics:* BMI, waist circumference, body-fat percentage; muscle strength (grip dynamometer, push-ups), endurance (VO₂max or timed run), flexibility (sit-and-reach), balance (one-leg stand).  *Standards:* WHO defines overweight as BMI ≥25 (both sexes) and obesity ≥30.  Waist cutoffs: >102 cm (40″) in men, >88 cm (35″) in women signal elevated risk (NCEP ATP III criteria).  Normative grip strength (healthy young adults): ~47 kg (men) vs ~30 kg (women) (declines with age).  *Cardio-respiratory fitness:* e.g. VO₂max (ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) “good” norms by age/sex vary (≈35–40 for middle-aged men, ≈30–35 women).  *Clinical thresholds:* Typical targets are BP <130/80 mmHg (AHA/ACC pre-HTN), LDL cholesterol <100 mg/dL, fasting glucose <100 mg/dL.  *Limitations:* BMI ignores muscle vs fat; ethnic differences in risk at given BMI; VO₂max requires exercise testing; many fitness tests lack universal norms.  

**Sociological Benchmarks:**  Population data reveal stark disparities.  For example, WHO reports *adolescent overweight* (5–19 yrs) at ~21% for boys vs 19% for girls (chart below), and *physical inactivity* at ~85% in girls vs 78% in boys (11–17 yrs).  Adults worldwide show ~43–44% overweight in both sexes.  However, low-income and certain cultural groups often have less healthy diets and activity: e.g. obesity is rising in lower-income countries; women in many societies have fewer exercise opportunities.  Education and income inversely correlate with obesity in high-income countries, and a lack of safe spaces diminishes activity.

**Standardized FIS Metrics (recommendation):**  Based on these findings and the (assumed) FIS Vision, we propose a core metric set for integration into FIS:

- **Sleep metrics:**  *Target:* 7–9 h/night (adult) plus quality.  *Measure:* Weekly average sleep duration and efficiency via actigraphy or validated sleep diaries.  *Threshold:* <7 h indicates risk; ≥7–8 h healthy.  *Rationale:* Strong links between <7 h sleep and obesity, CVD.  *Data:* Use wearable/smartphone data; supplement with sleep questionnaires (e.g. PSQI).  

- **Nutrition metrics:**  *Targets:* Daily servings of fruits/vegetables (≥5/day or ≥400 g) and fiber (≥25 g); macronutrient balance (e.g. ≤10% energy from sugar, ≤30% from fat).  *Measure:* Regular 24-h dietary recalls or a digital food log, combined into a Diet Quality Score (e.g. Healthy Eating Index).  *Thresholds:* E.g. fruit+veg ≥400 g; sugar <10%E.  *Rationale:* These reflect global dietary recommendations.  *Data:* Food frequency surveys or photo-assisted logging; national dietary surveys for benchmarking.  

- **Physical activity metrics:**  *Target:* ≥150 min/week MVPA + muscle-strengthening (or age-specific).  *Measure:* Accelerometer (steps/day, moderate/vigorous minutes) or validated questionnaire. Include at least 2 days/week of strength exercises.  *Thresholds:* 7,000–10,000 steps/day or 150–300 min MVPA/wk (adult); 60 min/day MVPA (children).  *Rationale:* Aligns with WHO and CDC guidelines.  *Data:* Wearables or smartphone sensors; ensure inclusion of occupational and transport activity.  

- **Fitness/Health metrics:**  *Composition:* BMI and waist circumference (with sex-specific cutoffs); body-fat % (if possible).  *Cardio:* VO₂max estimate (from submaximal tests or algorithms).  *Strength:* Grip strength or performance tests (e.g. push-up count).  *Flexibility/Balance:* Standard tests (sit-and-reach, one-leg stand time).  *Clinical:* BP, fasting glucose and lipids from medical records.  *Thresholds:* BMI 18.5–24.9 normal (both sexes); waist >102/88 cm high risk; VO₂max normative by age/sex; grip ~5th-95th percentile norms.  *Rationale:* These are validated health predictors.  

- **Sociodemographic metrics:** Track disparities by sex, age, income, education.  E.g. proportion meeting all targets, by subgroups.  *Rationale:* FIS must address equity; e.g. ensure female and low-SES populations meet guidelines.  

Each metric should include *data collection protocols*: e.g. “Sleep: two-week actigraphy and daily log”; “Diet: 3x24-h recalls”; “PA: 7-day accelerometry”; “Fitness: annual health check with BMI, BP, lab tests, and biennial fitness tests (VO₂max or walk test, grip).” 

**Limitations & Assumptions:**  Guidelines often lack sex-specific cutoffs, so FIS should apply or adjust these (e.g. different caloric needs).  We assume global adult norms, recognizing regional diet/activity differences.  Measurement feasibility varies: high-tech (sleep labs, DEXA) may be impractical; FIS may rely on scalable proxies (wearables, questionnaires).  Some FIS Vision elements were unspecified (e.g. how to weight composite “fitness score”), so we assume equal emphasis on domains.  Gaps include understudied areas (e.g. mental resilience, which also affects fitness but lacks standard metrics).  

**Conclusion:**  In sum, authoritative standards in sleep, nutrition, and activity provide clear quantitative targets by age (and, to a lesser extent, by sex).  The FIS should harmonize these into a unified framework: e.g. incorporate ≥7 h sleep, ≥5 veg/fruit servings, ≥150 min PA, BMI 18.5–24.9, etc., with adjustments for sex and age.  Collecting these via digital tools and regular health assessments will allow FIS to benchmark fitness and identify risks.  All sources used are authoritative (WHO fact sheets, CDC, peer-reviewed guidelines).
